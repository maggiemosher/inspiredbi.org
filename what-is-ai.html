AI Basics

The inner workings of AI, one concept at a time

Artificial intelligence can feel like a black box. It isn't. Underneath every chatbot and AI assistant are five ideas that build on each other. Read them in order and the whole field clicks into place. Each stop has a plain-English explanation, a simple visual, and a short, pre-vetted video from a trusted source (IBM and Google Cloud).

1

Generative AI (GenAI)

The AI that creates net-new content
What it is

Most software retrieves things that already exist — it looks up a record and shows it to you. Generative AI is different: it produces brand-new content that never existed before. You give it a prompt ("write a welcome email," "draft a rubric," "make an image of a wolf mascot"), and it generates original text, code, images, audio, or video on demand.

Plain version: It learned patterns from an enormous amount of human-made examples, and now it can mix those patterns into something new — the way a jazz musician who has heard thousands of songs can improvise a fresh one.

▶ IBM Technology — "What is Generative AI?"

2

Large Language Models (LLMs)

The engine behind text-based AI
How it works

An LLM is the "brain" that powers text tools like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. At its core it does one surprisingly simple thing extremely well: it predicts the next word (technically, the next "token") in a sequence, over and over, until a full response is built.

To do that, it first breaks your text into tokens (words or word-pieces), then uses patterns learned from billions of sentences to estimate which token should come next — and how likely each option is.

Plain version: It's the world's most sophisticated autocomplete. Because it has read so much human writing, its "best next word" guesses string together into fluent, useful answers.

▶ IBM Technology — "What are Large Language Models (LLMs)?"

3

Large Multimodal Models (LMMs)

AI that sees, hears, and reads at once
The evolution

Early AI worked in one "language" at a time — text only, or images only. Multimodal models understand and create across many formats simultaneously: text, images, audio, and video, all in a single conversation.

You can hand an LMM a photo of a syllabus, ask a spoken question about it, and get back a written summary plus a chart — because it processes every format in one shared "mind."

Plain version: If an LLM reads, an LMM reads and looks and listens — then answers in whatever format is most useful.

▶ Google Cloud Tech — "Multimodal Generative AI explained"

4

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Grounding AI in YOUR trusted knowledge
Why it matters

An LLM only knows what it learned during training — so it can "hallucinate," confidently inventing facts. RAG fixes this by looking up real, approved information first, then handing that to the model as context before it writes an answer.

For a university, this is the key to safety: connect the AI to your policies, catalog, and handbook, and it answers from those documents instead of guessing. Sensitive data stays inside your secure source.

Plain version: Open-book instead of from-memory. The AI checks your official documents first, then explains the answer in its own words — with far fewer mistakes.

▶ IBM Technology — "What is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)?"

5

Agentic AI (AI Agents)

From answering questions to getting things done
The frontier

A chatbot is passive — it waits for you and replies. An AI agent is active: give it a goal and it plans the steps, uses tools, and works through a multi-step task toward checkpoints, only pausing when it needs you.

Ask an agent to "build a study schedule from this syllabus and add it to my calendar," and it will read the file, draft the plan, and take the actions — coordinating several tools to reach the goal.

Plain version: A chatbot is a knowledgeable advisor; an agent is a capable assistant who actually carries out the plan, step by step.

▶ IBM Technology — "What are AI Agents?"

Higher Education Application

Putting it to work at Cheyney

Understanding the concepts is step one. Here's what they mean in practice — separated into guidance for staff and guidance for working with students.

🎓

For University Staff

Bias check before you rely on any output: Who is missing? Whose perspective dominates?
  • Data privacy first. Never paste student records, grades, or identifiable data into consumer AI tools. Use institution-approved, FERPA-aligned platforms and prefer RAG systems that keep data inside Cheyney's secure sources.
  • Redesign the syllabus for AI. State clearly where AI is encouraged, permitted with citation, or off-limits — assignment by assignment. Design tasks that reward process, reflection, and voice.
  • Evaluate AI bias. Interrogate every output: whose perspective dominates the answer, whose is absent, and what stereotypes might be baked in? Model this scrutiny for your students.
  • Treat AI as a co-creator, not an oracle. Use it to draft, brainstorm, and get unstuck — then apply your expertise to verify, revise, and own the final work.
🐺

Working with Students

Goal: students who direct AI with judgment — not students who outsource thinking to it.
  • Cultivate a growth mindset. Frame AI as a tool for iteration and feedback, not a shortcut. Reward the revision, the questions asked, and the reasoning — not just a polished result.
  • Teach verification. Show students how to fact-check AI output against primary sources, spot hallucinations, and demand citations before trusting an answer.
  • Executive-function coaching. Use AI to break big projects into steps, build study schedules, and scaffold time management — supporting focus and follow-through.
  • Self-regulated learning. Encourage students to use AI as a patient tutor for practice questions, explanations, and reflection, building independence and metacognition over time.
