K-12 Educators

The pause heard 'round the country

What's New · September 2026

The biggest classroom AI story of the year landed on September 2: New York City Public Schools imposed a one-year moratorium on all student-facing generative AI for 2-K through 8th grade, covering nearly 600,000 students, and banned companion chatbots in every grade. Teachers keep full access to AI for planning and operations, and exceptions remain for assistive tech, multilingual learners, and CS pathways. High schools get two 45-minute AI literacy modules per year plus supervised pilots of Quill, Edia, Brisk, Playlab, and Intel AI-Ready Schools, each capped at 10–20 minutes a week. The timing matters because Common Sense Media's August survey of 1,017 teens found 70% use AI for schoolwork, yet only 30% have ever had a teacher discuss safe use, and 28% say rules change from teacher to teacher. For teachers who are keeping AI, professional development is front-loaded this month: Google's free AI Educator Series now adds new ISTE-aligned modules the first Wednesday of every month, and a 10-hour virtual Badge-a-thon runs September 19 with live badging. Best move this week: whatever your district's stance, put your AI expectation in writing on every assignment sheet — it's the inconsistency, not the tool, that students say confuses them.

School Leaders

Two cities, two very different playbooks

What's New · September 2026

The same week produced two opposite models of district AI governance. New York City chose a moratorium: no student-facing generative AI through 8th grade, screen-time caps of 30 minutes a day for grades 3–5 and 45 minutes for 6–8, a mandatory review of every tech tool for "mission-critical" learning value, and a Technology in Schools Coalition that will report recommendations after a year of study. Pilots reach at most 50,000 high schoolers — about 5% of enrollment. Washington, D.C. chose a rulebook instead: on September 1, OSSE released a non-mandatory model policy built on a red-yellow-green "stoplight" framework. Green-lights lesson drafting, scheduling, and attendance analysis; yellow requires substantial human oversight for grading and reviewing student work; red bars AI outright from IEP determinations, student discipline, teacher evaluations, and physical surveillance. D.C. built it because its own February survey found only 45% of local districts had staff AI policies and 34% had student policies. Nationally, 35 states have published K-12 AI guidance, nearly all nonbinding, and 60% of educators still call their district's AI policy unclear. Your move: whichever direction your board leans, D.C.'s three-color list of prohibited high-stakes uses is a ready-made appendix for any existing policy.

What's New · August 2026
